---
title: "I Said Yes to a Day We Might Waste"
description: "A teammate wanted one day to automate a tedious part of our front-end work. We had no proof it would work, which turned out to be a manageable problem."
date: "2026-11-17"
---

For a while, part of our front-end work involved preparing images for client sites. An image would arrive in roughly the same condition as luggage after a long flight: technically intact, but with some questions attached. It might be enormous, oddly cropped, named `final-final-2.jpg`, or all three.

We needed several versions for different parts of the site, so somebody had to resize it, crop it, export it, check it, and occasionally stare at it long enough to decide whether the important part was the person’s face or the decorative fern behind them.

None of this was especially difficult. That was almost the problem. Difficult work gets discussed. Tedious work quietly rents a desk and starts attending standup.

One of the developers on the team suggested that we spend a day building a small tool to handle the normal cases. Drop in an image, choose a couple of options, and get the versions the site needed. The weird files would still need attention, because there is no script powerful enough to understand every choice a client has ever made in Photoshop.

The proposal was reasonable. It was also unproven. We did not know whether one day would produce something useful, whether the edge cases would swallow the whole idea, or whether we would end up with a new tool that required more care than the chore it replaced. We already had client work scheduled. Giving the experiment a day meant moving a lower-priority bit of visual polish.

I said yes anyway, with an agreement that we would look at it before signing off and decide whether it had earned any more time.

This was not a dramatic act of leadership. Nobody applauded. I mostly spent the morning wondering whether I had traded visible progress for a script that would eventually be abandoned in a folder called `tools-old`.

By the afternoon, it worked on the ordinary files. It did not work on a panoramic image that seemed to have been photographed from a moving canoe, and it made one transparent logo look like it had been printed on a black shower curtain. Still, the common case was common enough. We kept the tool, fixed a few rough edges later, and continued handling the strange files by hand.

What made the decision useful was not confidence in the outcome. We had very little of that. It was the size of the bet. One day, one delayed task, and a clear moment to look at what we had learned. If the tool had failed, we would have lost a day and gained a better understanding of why the work resisted automation. Annoying, perhaps, but survivable.

I think this is where being careful with commitments can become a little too careful. Once you get good at seeing hidden costs, every new idea arrives wearing a price tag. You can become so determined to avoid waste that you require proof before allowing an experiment whose whole purpose is to produce the proof.

The better question is often what you are willing to spend to find out. Maybe it is a day. Maybe it is a small budget, one meeting, or a trial with a date on the calendar when everyone agrees to stop and review it. The boundary matters because “let’s try this for a while” has a habit of becoming a permanent responsibility with a temporary name tag.

Of course, a time limit does not make every idea good. A one-week experiment can still be seven days devoted to something nobody needs. It should answer a real question, and the answer should affect what happens next. Otherwise, you have created a tiny project with excellent branding.

We could have kept preparing every image by hand. The process worked, in the same way that carrying groceries one item at a time works. Saying yes to a day of uncertainty gave us a chance to find a better way without pretending we already knew the result.
