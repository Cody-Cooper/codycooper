---
title: "I Agreed to Keep an Eye on the Inbox"
date: "2026-11-10"
description: "Keeping an eye on a client inbox sounded like checking it occasionally. The inbox had a more ambitious interpretation."
---

At the end of a client launch call, someone asked whether I could keep an eye on the new support inbox for a couple of weeks.

I said sure. We had built the website, I knew how the forms worked, and checking an inbox sounded smaller than almost anything else we had discussed that day. It was barely a task. You keep an eye on the weather. You keep an eye on a pot of water. Neither usually creates a ticket with your name on it.

The first few messages were exactly what I expected. A confirmation email went to spam. Someone found a typo. One person managed to submit the contact form without a phone number, despite the phone number being required. Websites are very respectful of rules until a visitor arrives.

I fixed the technical issues and replied to let people know. Since I was already in the inbox, I also forwarded a question about an invoice and answered someone who could not remember which email address they had used for an account. By the end of the week, messages were being assigned to me automatically.

This was efficient in the limited sense that nobody had to decide where they should go.

The second week brought a request to change an order, a complaint about a delivery, and a long message from someone who wanted to know whether the company planned to offer a product it had never sold. I could not answer any of those, but I had become the only person consistently opening the inbox. Each time I found the right person and passed a message along, I made the arrangement look slightly more intentional than it was.

Then an email marked urgent sat for most of a day. It was a billing issue. I had seen it that morning, decided it belonged with the client’s finance team, and assumed someone on their side would pick it up. The client assumed I was triaging the inbox and would send anything important to the right person.

We had both kept an eye on it. The email remained impressively unhelped.

Nobody had been unreasonable. The client needed coverage while the new site settled in. I had agreed because technical questions were likely, and answering those quickly made sense. The problem was that “keep an eye on it” described attention without saying what should happen after I noticed something.

We had never agreed how often I would check, which messages belonged to me, or whether I should answer them, assign them, or simply confirm the inbox was receiving mail. We also had no definition of urgent and no decision-maker for questions that had nothing to do with the website. The request had sounded too small to deserve that discussion. The missing answers did not stay small.

We reset the arrangement. I would review the inbox once each morning through the end of the week and handle technical problems caused by the site. A named person on the client’s team would own the inbox, route everything else, and decide when something needed immediate attention. After Friday, new website bugs would come to us through the normal support process.

The new plan took about ten minutes to agree on. We skipped the workflow diagram, the ticketing-system migration, and the committee with a name. We wrote down the result, the duration, and the person who could say, “This part is mine.”

That last part matters because vague ownership tends to flow toward the person who responds first. Helpful people become the default destination. Once they have solved a few problems, everyone else has reasonable evidence that the arrangement is working. The work continues arriving, even if nobody remembers making the assignment permanent.

I still agree to small favors without turning them into contract negotiations. Most requests do not need five meetings and a project charter. I try to notice when a favor includes an ongoing verb, though. Monitor, maintain, coordinate, support, and follow up all have a way of returning tomorrow.

Before I say yes, I want to know what finishing looks like and who owns whatever happens next. Otherwise, “keep an eye on it” can become a job description written entirely in lowercase.
