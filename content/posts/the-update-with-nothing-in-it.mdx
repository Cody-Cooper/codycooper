---
title: "The Update With Nothing in It"
date: "2026-11-14"
description: "Nothing had changed, so I skipped the update. An hour later, my boss was wondering whether the team was okay."
---

During a sustained investigation, I made what felt like a very reasonable decision: I skipped an update because nothing had changed.

We were waiting on results from an external forensic tool. Until those results came back, there was no new scope, no new finding, and no useful decision for leadership to make. I had been sending updates every two hours, but the 2:00 update arrived with nothing to put in it. Sending a message to announce that I had no message seemed like an excellent use of nobody’s time.

So I kept working.

At 4:00, we were still waiting. I skipped that update too, now with the added confidence of someone who had already made the same decision once and faced no immediate consequences.

Around 5:00, my boss called. His first question was whether everything was okay with the team.

That caught me off guard. I thought I had saved him from two interruptions. From his side, I had sent updates on a predictable schedule for two days and then disappeared. He did not know we were calmly waiting on a result. He knew only that the person who had been checking in every two hours had stopped checking in.

Silence had supplied its own update, and it was considerably more dramatic than the one I had declined to send.

I had confused a lack of new findings with a lack of useful information. The useful information was that the plan had not changed. The team was still working. The expected result was still expected, and I still intended to call again at the next scheduled time. None of that was exciting, but leadership did not need excitement. They needed to know whether the clock and the plan were still real.

A no-change update can be short. It should be short. In this case, a few sentences would have done the job: there had been no material change since the last update, we were still waiting on the external result, the team was monitoring, and the next update would arrive at a specific time.

That message does more than report status. It keeps everyone from inventing an explanation for the gap. Without it, a leader has to wonder whether the team is stuck, whether the situation got worse, or whether the promised update was simply forgotten. People are very capable of filling silence, and they rarely fill it with “I’m sure everything is proceeding normally.”

The schedule also gives leadership somewhere to put their questions. If they know another update is coming at 4:00, they can wait until 4:00 unless something requires an answer sooner. If updates arrive whenever the incident lead happens to remember, every new question becomes a reasonable reason to call. The response team then loses time answering check-ins caused by the absence of check-ins, which is an impressively circular way to stay busy.

This does not mean sending the same paragraph every hour until someone begs you to stop. The cadence should match the pace of the incident. Early in a fast-moving response, an hour may be a long time. During recovery or while waiting on a slow external process, updates can spread out. The important part is saying when the next one will come and communicating any change to that schedule instead of quietly wandering away from it.

The schedule should also break when the situation changes in a way leadership would want to know before the next update. A new affected system, a failed containment step, or a commitment that will be missed should not wait for the clock. The cadence handles the ordinary gaps. It does not turn a 4:00 update into an appointment the bad news must respect.

After that phone call, I stopped treating “nothing changed” as a reason to skip an update. Sometimes it was the entire update. I could send it in less time than it took my boss to wonder where I had gone, decide the silence might mean something, and call to make sure the team had not fallen into a hole.

The update I almost sent would have taken about twenty seconds. The phone call my silence created took longer, and it began with a question about whether we were okay.
