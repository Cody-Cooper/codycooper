title: "I’m Known for Saying No at Work"

---

I have a reputation at work for saying no, which probably sounds like a strange thing to be pleased about. Bring me a new idea or ask me to take something on and I’m likely to have a few questions before I agree. Why are we doing this? Does it actually need to happen? What are we already working on that would have to wait? Sometimes I’ll ask what happens if we just don’t do it, which is apparently a less popular question.

I’m comfortable with that reputation because I want people to be able to rely on me when I do agree to something. If I tell you I can get something done by Friday, you should be able to plan around that. You shouldn’t have to spend Thursday afternoon checking whether I remembered, or have a backup ready because there’s a decent chance I’ll ask for another week.

That’s easy enough to agree with in principle. It gets harder when somebody is standing in front of you asking for help and you know they’d really like you to say yes.

## Figuring out what I’m agreeing to

Most requests sound pretty reasonable when you first hear them. Somebody needs you to look over a document, sit in on a meeting, or add one more thing to something you’re already doing. You want to help, and it feels a little ridiculous to make a whole discussion out of what sounds like a small favor. So you say sure, go back to your desk, and remember the other things you also said sure to.

The problem is that “take a look at this” can mean anything from checking a paragraph to reviewing an entire document and writing substantial feedback. A meeting might only take an hour, but you may need another hour to prepare for it. None of that makes the request unreasonable. It does make it worth finding out what somebody actually needs before promising you can do it.

The person asking probably doesn’t know everything else you have going on, either. They know what they need and when they need it. You’re the one who knows you already have two deadlines that week and barely enough time to meet them. I don’t think it’s fair to agree anyway and then act like they should have known you were too busy.

This is where I think people get caught up in whether they *can* make something happen. Sure, you could stay late, rush through something else, or finish it at home. Sometimes the work is important enough to justify that. But if that’s how you’re fitting in every new request, you’re agreeing to more work than you actually have time for. Being willing to do it doesn’t fix the schedule.

Before I take something on, I want to know where it’s going to fit. If something else needs to move, that should be part of the conversation, especially if somebody is already waiting on it. They made their plans based on an answer I gave them too.

## Giving somebody an answer they can use

I’d much rather hear “I can’t have that Friday, but I can get it to you Tuesday” when I first ask than find out on Friday that it isn’t happening. Tuesday might be too late, but at least I have time to do something about it. I can ask somebody else, cut back on what I need, or explain the delay to whoever is waiting on me. If you tell me Friday works, I’m probably going to stop looking for other options.

That’s how I try to handle requests myself. If I can review the first section today but won’t have time for the whole document, I can say that. If Wednesday works and Tuesday doesn’t, I can offer Wednesday. If I can take on something new only by moving an existing deadline, I need to say which one. The other person might decide that trade is worth making, or they might decide their request can wait. Either way, we’ve actually figured something out.

There’s also a point where the answer is just no. Sometimes there isn’t a smaller version that makes sense, and sometimes I don’t think the thing needs doing in the first place. It’s worth being able to explain why. Asking whether the work is useful is a reasonable question; making somebody defend every little request because you enjoy being difficult would get old very quickly.

I should probably make that distinction given that I’m writing a book called *Default: No*. You could read the title and reasonably assume I’m recommending that you become the most irritating person in your office. I’m talking about taking a moment to consider a request before agreeing to it. Usually that takes a few seconds, and when the work matters and I have room for it, saying yes is easy.

I don’t expect people to be thrilled every time I turn something down. If they needed help and I can’t give it, they’re allowed to be disappointed. I’d still rather have that conversation while they have time to make other arrangements than agree because I don’t want an awkward moment.

Being known for saying no is fine with me. I’m more concerned about what happens after I say yes, when somebody goes back to their own work assuming I’ll take care of what we discussed. I want them to be right about that.
