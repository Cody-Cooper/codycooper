---
title: "The Ticket Said Urgent. It Meant Thursday."
description: "An urgent client request arrived on Monday morning. One question turned it into a perfectly manageable Wednesday task."
date: "2026-11-19"
---

An urgent ticket arrived on a Monday morning while I was fixing the mobile navigation on a client’s site. The subject line said the homepage was wrong, which is a sentence that can move a developer’s coffee from one side of the desk to the other without being touched.

I opened the ticket. The homepage was not down, broken, offensive, or on fire. The client wanted to replace the main image and headline with material for an upcoming campaign. They had attached the copy, a mockup, and six images with filenames that suggested each one had, at some point, been the final choice.

The work looked straightforward, although web development has trained me to treat that thought as a minor electrical smell. I stopped what I was doing, pulled down the latest changes, and started checking the new assets.

Then I noticed the campaign page was still in draft. Nothing linked to it. The current homepage was doing exactly what it was supposed to do, and the campaign itself did not start for another week.

So I asked when somebody needed to see the change.

Thursday afternoon, they said. There was an internal review at two. It was Monday at ten in the morning.

I had already begun rearranging my day around a deadline that did not exist. The navigation fix was half done, another developer was waiting on it, and I had mentally promoted a homepage update ahead of both. The only new information driving that choice was the word urgent, written in capital letters by a ticketing system with no apparent concern for my blood pressure.

I told the client I could have the update ready Wednesday, which would leave time for them to review it before the meeting. They were happy with that. I went back to the navigation, finished the work already in progress, and handled the campaign the next day without anyone standing behind me ringing a small bell.

The client had not tried to mislead me. The review mattered to them, and they did not know how long our queue was or when someone would open the request. Marking it urgent was a reasonable attempt to make sure it did not disappear. The problem was that the label carried more emotion than scheduling information.

“Urgent” can mean the public site is giving customers the wrong price. It can mean a manager wants to review something on Thursday. It can also mean the requester has been worrying about the task since Friday and would feel much better if somebody acknowledged it. Those are all reasons to respond, but they are not reasons to make the same change to the plan.

A date helps, but the reason behind the date helps more. If another team cannot continue until a file arrives, waiting has a cost. If incorrect information is live, every hour exposes more people to it. If the work is for a meeting later in the week, there may be several good places to put it. The calendar only tells you when. The consequence tells you what the date is doing there.

This does not require turning every request into a deposition. Nobody wants to defend a homepage image before a panel of developers wearing reading glasses. Usually one ordinary question is enough: what happens if this is done tomorrow instead of today?

Sometimes the answer will justify dropping everything. A broken login, a bad price, or a form sending customer information to the wrong place deserves a different response from a draft page waiting for an internal review. Asking the question does not make the work less important. It gives the work the right place among everything else that is already important.

I still pay attention when somebody marks a request urgent. I just try not to let the adjective plan the day by itself. The ticket may be announcing an emergency. It may also be announcing Thursday.
